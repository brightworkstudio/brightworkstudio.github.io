1. The story in one paragraph

In the last hours of 31 August 1854, cholera erupted in the streets around Golden Square, Soho. Within about 250 yards of the corner of Cambridge Street and Broad Street there were more than five hundred fatal attacks in ten days, and most of the population fled within a week [1, p.38]. John Snow, a physician who already believed cholera was spread by contaminated water, suspected the popular street pump in Broad Street, examined its water on the evening of 3 September, obtained the death registers, and mapped the deaths against the pump [1, pp.38-40]. He put his findings to the parish Board of Guardians on the evening of Thursday 7 September; the pump handle was removed the next day [1, p.40]. The parish's own inquiry, driven by the curate Henry Whitehead, later traced the likely source to the cesspool of No. 40 Broad Street, next to the well, into which the washings from a sick infant had been thrown on 28-30 August [2, pp.91-92, 127]. The episode became the founding story of epidemiology, but the neat version (map, pump handle, outbreak stopped) is not quite what the sources say.

2. Verified timeline

Date (1854)EventSource
Late AugustA few cholera cases in the neighbourhood of Broad Street; the epidemic is already present elsewhere in London.[1, p.39]
28 AugAn infant at No. 40 Broad Street falls ill with diarrhoea; on 28, 29 and 30 August "considerable quantities of water containing the diluted dejections" of the child are thrown into the house cesspool, a few feet from the pump well.[2, p.92]
Night of 31 Aug - 1 SepThe outbreak proper begins, "extremely sudden"; 1 September is the single worst day (143 attacks by Snow's count, then 116 on the 2nd, 54 on the 3rd).[1, pp.39, 51]
Week ending 2 Sep89 cholera deaths registered in the sub-districts of Golden Square, Berwick Street and St Ann's, Soho: 6 in the first four days, 4 on Thursday 31 Aug, 79 on Friday and Saturday. Snow investigates the 83 deaths of the last three days.[1, p.39]
1-2 SepSnow later ascertains 197 deaths belonging to the outbreak on these two days alone.[1, p.41]
2 SepThe infant at No. 40 dies ("exhaustion after diarrhoea"; registered by the Registrar-General among the cholera deaths). Same day, at West End, Hampstead, a 59-year-old widow who had a bottle of Broad Street pump water brought to her by cart dies of cholera; her niece, who also drank it, dies in Islington.[2, p.92]; [1, p.44]
3 Sep (Sun), eveningSnow examines the pump water and finds "so little impurity in it of an organic nature" that he hesitates; over the next two days he sees varying amounts of "small white, flocculent particles".[1, p.39]
3-6 SepSnow's house-to-house inquiry: of the 83 deaths, 61 people were known to drink the pump water constantly or occasionally, 6 could not be traced, 6 reportedly did not drink it; 10 deaths were nearer another pump, but 5 of those families preferred Broad Street water and 3 were children at a school near the pump.[1, pp.39-40]
7 Sep (Thu), eveningSnow meets the Board of Guardians of St James's parish and presents his evidence.[1, p.40]
8 Sep (Fri)The pump handle is removed. Snow's own table shows attacks were already falling: 12 new attacks on the 8th, 11 on the 9th, 5, 5, then 1 on the 12th.[1, pp.40, 51-52]
Sep-Oct 1854The parish Cholera Inquiry Committee is formed; Whitehead's street-by-street investigation and the surveyor York's excavation of the No. 40 cesspool and well follow.[2, preface; pp.91-92]
April 1855Whitehead's investigation complete; up to then "the entrance of sewer or cesspool-drainage into the well in Broad Street was not proved". York's excavation then shows the cesspool "so choked and defective as to have allowed percolation into the soft black soil around, and thence into the well itself".[2, pp.91-92]
1855Snow publishes the second, "much enlarged" edition of On the Mode of Communication of Cholera, including the Broad Street map; the Committee publishes its Report.[1]; [2]

3. People and figures

  • John Snow (1813-1858), physician and anaesthetist, author of the 1849 and 1855 editions of On the Mode of Communication of Cholera [1, title page; 3].
  • Rev. Henry Whitehead, curate of St Luke's, Berwick Street; member of the parish Cholera Inquiry Committee whose "minute and pains-taking investigation" of Broad Street is appended to the Committee's report [2, preface; p.127].
  • Mr York, the Committee's secretary and surveyor, who inspected the cesspool and drains of No. 40 and opened the well [2, preface].
  • Dr Rogers, medical attendant to the household at No. 40 [2, p.92]. Mr Huggins, brewery proprietor in Broad Street [1, p.42]. Mr Marshall, surgeon of Greek Street, who supplied several case histories [1, p.43]. Dr Fraser of Oakley Square, who reported the Hampstead case [1, p.44].
  • Scale: "upwards of five hundred fatal attacks of cholera in ten days" within 250 yards of the Cambridge Street corner [1, p.38]. The Committee counts 618 ascertained deaths of residents in its defined "cholera area" (population about 14,000; 440 per 10,000), plus 45 known non-residents, and estimates fatal attacks in the parish "probably not less than 700" [2, Report, sections on mortality].
  • The three natural experiments Snow used: the Poland Street workhouse (535 inmates, own well and Grand Junction supply, only 5 cholera deaths); the Broad Street brewery (about 70 men, own deep well and free malt liquor, no deaths); the percussion-cap factory at 37 Broad Street (about 200 workers, two tubs of pump water kept for drinking, 18 dead) [1, pp.42-43].

4. Quotes (verbatim, with location)

"The most terrible outbreak of cholera which ever occurred in this kingdom, is probably that which took place in Broad Street, Golden Square, and the adjoining streets, a few weeks ago." [1, p.38]
"I had an interview with the Board of Guardians of St. James's parish, on the evening of Thursday, 7th September, and represented the above circumstances to them. In consequence of what I said, the handle of the pump was removed on the following day." [1, p.40]
"...the attacks had so far diminished before the use of the water was stopped, that it is impossible to decide whether the well still contained the cholera poison in an active state, or whether, from some cause, the water had become free from it." [1, pp.51-52]
"At the time of Dr. Snow's inquiry in the early part of September 1854, and indeed up to April 1855, when Mr. Whitehead's investigation was complete, the entrance of sewer or cesspool-drainage into the well in Broad Street was not proved. All the evidence was to the opposite effect." [2, pp.91-92]

Note on the popular quote: no contemporary source we opened has Snow saying he "took the handle off the pump" himself; his own account credits the Board of Guardians. Treat any first-person handle quote as later embroidery unless a source is produced.

5. Open questions and disputed points

  1. Did removing the handle stop the outbreak? Snow himself says it is "impossible to decide", because attacks had already fallen from 143 (1 Sep) to 12 (8 Sep) before the handle came off [1, pp.51-52]. Modern historians make the same point and warn against the "myth" that the map led to the handle and the handle ended the epidemic [4, 5]. Safe wording on air: the handle was removed as the outbreak was already subsiding; its removal probably prevented a second wave rather than ending the first.
  2. Was the map the discovery tool? Snow's inquiry in early September used the Registrar's death lists and house visits; the published dot map appears in the 1855 book [1, "Map 1"]. Brody and colleagues argue the map illustrated a conclusion Snow had already reached rather than producing it [4].
  3. What was the index case? The Committee (via Whitehead) points to the infant at No. 40, but records the doubt "as to the real nature of the attack": the child's history and Dr Rogers's opinion suggested ordinary infantile diarrhoea, while the Registrar-General listed it as cholera [2, p.92]. Whitehead also notes the mother "emphatically denies" the child ever drank pump water, and that the father died of cholera on 8 September, almost the last case in the street [2, p.127].
  4. How many died? Figures depend on the boundary and the counting: 500+ fatal attacks in ten days near the pump (Snow); 618 resident deaths in the Committee's "cholera area"; "not less than 700" fatal attacks in the parish over 17 weeks (Committee) [1, p.38; 2]. Quote whichever you use with its definition.
  5. Official reception. The General Board of Health's own inquiry rejected Snow's water conclusion in favour of an atmospheric explanation, even though one of its investigators saw "vibriones" in cholera stools; see Paneth et al. for the rivalry between the official and unofficial investigations [5].

7. How to use this

  • Cold open: the Hampstead widow, aged 59, who had Broad Street water carted to her because she liked the taste, and died two days later miles from Soho [1, p.44].
  • Most surprising verified detail: the brewery next to the pump lost nobody; the percussion-cap factory across the street, with two tubs of pump water for its 200 workers, lost 18 [1, pp.42-43].
  • Biggest hedge: do not say the pump handle ended the outbreak; Snow did not claim it, and his own numbers show the decline began a week earlier [1, pp.51-52].
