Sample boxing script: "The Night Nobody Saw Coming"

Documentary-style YouTube script sample, prepared as a portfolio piece by Brightwork Studio. Excerpt of about 800 words (roughly 5 to 6 minutes of narration). Full scripts run around 1,500 words / 15 minutes and follow the same structure: cold open, setup, turning point, payoff.

COLD OPEN

June 1st, 2019. Madison Square Garden. Anthony Joshua walks to the ring for his American debut carrying three world heavyweight titles, a 22 and 0 record, and the kind of physique that makes people forget boxing is not decided in the gym.

Across from him stands a man the crowd barely recognises. Two hundred and sixty-eight pounds. Soft around the middle. A replacement opponent booked on a few weeks notice.

Ninety minutes later, that man is on his knees in the corner, crying, with all three belts stacked in front of him.

This is the story of how Andy Ruiz Jr. pulled off the biggest heavyweight upset since Buster Douglas, and what happened when he tried to do it twice.

PART ONE: THE OPPONENT WHO WAS NEVER MEANT TO BE THERE

Ruiz was not supposed to be in New York that night. The fight was built for Jarrell Miller, a loud Brooklyn heavyweight who had spent months talking his way into the biggest payday of his life. Then the drug tests came back. Not one. Several. Miller was gone, the date was fixed, and the promoter needed a name in a hurry.

Ruiz put his hand up. He had one loss on his record, a close decision to Joseph Parker in 2016, and he had just beaten Alexander Dimitrenko in April. But nobody was talking about his record. They were talking about his body. Commentators joked about it. Fans joked about it. The betting odds treated the fight as an exhibition.

What the jokes missed was the thing that actually mattered: Ruiz had some of the fastest hands in the division. Trained in the Mexican style since childhood, he threw combinations, not single shots. And he had a chin that had never been seriously tested because nobody had ever managed to hurt him.

PART TWO: ROUND THREE

For two rounds, the script held. Joshua controlled distance behind the jab, Ruiz walked forward and ate it. Then, early in the third, Joshua landed the left hook he had been setting up, and Ruiz went down.

Here is where most replacement opponents stay down, or survive by holding. Ruiz did neither. He got up, and when Joshua came in to finish, Ruiz caught him with a short right hand high on the head. Joshua reeled. Ruiz followed with a burst of punches, and the champion dropped.

The Garden went silent, then loud in a completely different way. Joshua beat the count, but he was on unsteady legs, and Ruiz put him down a second time before the bell.

One round. Three knockdowns. The entire shape of the heavyweight division changed in three minutes.

PART THREE: THE FINISH

Joshua survived the fourth, fifth and sixth, but he was fighting on memory. In the seventh, Ruiz landed again and Joshua went down twice more. After the fourth knockdown, Joshua walked to a neutral corner, spat out his mouthpiece, and did not answer the referee clearly. Michael Griffin waved it off at one minute and twenty-seven seconds of round seven.

Andy Ruiz Jr., the stand-in, was the unified heavyweight champion of the world. The first fighter of Mexican descent ever to hold the heavyweight crown.

PART FOUR: SIX MONTHS

The rematch clause gave Joshua the right to run it back, and he took it: December 7th, 2019, in Diriyah, Saudi Arabia.

Joshua rebuilt himself for one purpose. He came in at two hundred and thirty-seven pounds, his lightest in years, and he stopped trying to be a wrecking ball. Jab, move, jab, clinch, reset.

Ruiz came in at two hundred and eighty-three pounds. Later he would admit what everyone could see: three months of celebrating, and the training camp only really started when it was too late.

There were no knockdowns. Joshua boxed every minute of twelve rounds from the outside and won a wide unanimous decision. The belts went back. The fairy tale lasted one hundred and eighty-nine days.

CLOSE

Boxing loves a Cinderella story, and it rarely lets one last. But that is not why people still watch the first Ruiz fight. They watch it because on one night, everything the sport uses to judge a fighter by sight turned out to be wrong, and the only thing that counted was what happened when the two men actually hit each other.
