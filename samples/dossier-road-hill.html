Research dossier: the Road Hill House murder, Wiltshire, 1860

Prepared for: a narrative true-crime podcast (assumed brief: one 40-50 minute episode on the murder of Francis Saville Kent, the 1860 investigation and the 1865 confession).
Prepared by: Brightwork Studio (HireThomas, Inc.), 28 September 2026. AI tools were used in the research and drafting; every claim below was checked by us against the source opened, and every source listed was actually opened at the link given.
What this is: the $40 product, as delivered: a verified timeline, the key people, verbatim quotes with their location, the open or disputed points, and a numbered source list. It is not a script.

1. The story in one paragraph

On the morning of Saturday 30 June 1860 the three-year-old son of Samuel Kent, a factory inspector living at Road Hill House on the Wiltshire-Somerset border, was missing from his cot. His body was found in the servants' privy in the grounds, the throat cut so deeply that the head was nearly severed [1, p.5]. The house had been locked the night before; the housemaid found the drawing-room door, shutters and window slightly open at five past six [1, pp.5-6]. A coroner's inquest returned "Wilful murder against some person or persons unknown" [1, p.8]. Scotland Yard's Inspector Whicher had the sixteen-year-old half-sister, Constance Kent, arrested on a warrant; the magistrates released her on her father's bail and the case against her collapsed, to loud press criticism of the detective [1, pp.4, 8, 19]. The nursemaid, Elizabeth Gough, was then arrested and discharged in her turn [1, pp.32-34]. Nearly five years later, on 25 April 1865, Constance Kent walked into Bow Street with a clergyman from Brighton and gave herself up on a written confession [2, Chronicle pp.47-49]. At Salisbury Assizes on 20 July 1865 she pleaded guilty before Mr Justice Willes, who wept as he passed sentence of death; the sentence was commuted to penal servitude for life [2, Trials pp.228-229 and following]. The case is remembered as the making, and near-breaking, of the Victorian detective; the sources show how much of the 1860 evidence rested on a missing nightdress and a house that could not decide whether it had been broken into.

3. People

  • Francis Saville Kent, the victim, "a pretty little boy" of about three [1, p.5]; the 1865 charge names him "Francis Saville Kent" [2, Chronicle p.49].
  • Samuel Kent, the father; four children by a first wife ("three girls and one boy"), the two youngest of them being Constance and a brother, plus younger children by the second Mrs Kent [1, p.4]. Represented at the inquest by D. Rodway, solicitor, Trowbridge [1, p.5].
  • Constance Emily (Emilie) Kent, "sixteen years of age" at the inquest [1, p.7]; "about twenty-one" at Bow Street [2, Chronicle p.48].
  • Elizabeth Gough, nursemaid, 22, "the first person suspected of complicity" [1, pp.33-34].
  • Inspector Jonathan Whicher (spelled "Witcher" in the 1860 pamphlet), Metropolitan detective force, with Inspector Williamson [1, p.8; 2, Chronicle p.48].
  • Superintendent Foley, Trowbridge police; George Silvester, coroner; Rev. Edward Peacock, jury foreman [1, p.5].
  • Rev. Arthur Douglas Wagner, St Paul's, Brighton, to whom she "revealed her guilt" [2, Chronicle pp.48-51].
  • Sir Thomas Henry, chief magistrate, Bow Street; Mr Justice Willes; Mr Coleridge QC for the defence [2].

5. Open questions and disputed points

  1. Exact dates of the 1860 arrest and discharge. The 1860 pamphlet dates the arrest only as "yesterday" relative to its own printing [1, p.8] and the discharge as "on her father's bail" [1, p.19]. Standard accounts give 16 July and 27 July 1860; we have not confirmed those from an opened source. A newspaper of the week (e.g. The Times, 17 and 28 July 1860) would settle it.
  2. Which night? The 1865 charge says "on the night of the 29th of June" [2, p.48-49]; the indictment and the judge say "the 30th of June" [2, p.228-229]. Both are right (the night of 29-30 June); a script should pick one and say so.
  3. The drawing-room window. Cox swore the window could not be opened from outside without damage, yet found it open in the morning [1, pp.5-6]. The 1860 sources treat this as evidence of an inside job; whether it was staged, and by whom, is not settled in what we opened.
  4. Cause of death. Contemporary opinion split between the throat wound and prior suffocation; Stapleton argues hard against suffocation [3, ch. III]. The inquest surgeons' own words were not in the extracts we read; the full inquest depositions [1, pp.5-8] should be read in full before scripting the medical passage.
  5. The nightdress. Whicher's case rested on a nightdress that could not be accounted for; the airing witness counted three [1, pp.11-12]. The 1865 confession does not, in the extracts read, explain it. Worth a careful passage, not a confident one.
  6. Elizabeth Gough's arrest dates are given only as "Saturday last" and "Friday evening" in the pamphlet's press extracts [1, p.33]; confirm from The Times of October 1860 before stating them.
  7. Later life. The commutation is stated [2]; release date and later identity are not in the opened sources and should not be asserted from memory.
